Gradient descent is the engine that powers neural network training. Imagine a hilly landscape where the height of the hills represents the "loss" or error of our model. Our goal is to find the lowest point in this landscape—the global minimum.

In mathematical terms, we want to minimize a loss function $L(\theta)$ with respect to its parameters $\theta$. We do this by taking small steps in the direction of the negative gradient:

$$ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t) $$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate.

### Understanding the Hyperparameters

#### 1. Learning Rate ($\eta$)

If the learning rate is too small, the ball takes forever to reach the bottom, and might get stuck in a flat region. 
If it is too large, the ball might overshoot the minimum and bounce wildly across the valley, failing to converge or even diverging to infinity. 
* **Try this:** Set the surface to **Rosenbrock Valley**, turn off Momentum, and set the Learning Rate to `0.01`. The loss will quickly hit `NaN` (divergence).

#### 2. Momentum ($\gamma$)

Momentum helps accelerate gradients in the right direction, dampening oscillations. It accumulates an exponentially decaying moving average of past gradients and continues to move in their direction:

$$ v_{t+1} = \gamma v_t + \eta \nabla L(\theta_t) $$
$$ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - v_{t+1} $$

* **Try this:** On the **Ravine** surface, a standard SGD optimizer bounces side-to-side incredibly slowly. Turn on Momentum, and watch how it cuts through the ravine efficiently by carrying its velocity forward!
