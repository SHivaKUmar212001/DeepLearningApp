We now have a U-Net that can perfectly predict and remove noise to generate photorealistic images. 

But if we just give it random noise, we have no control over what it generates. It might generate a dog, a car, or a landscape. How do we *force* it to generate exactly what we type into a text box, like "A cute cat"?

## Conditioning

To do this, we pass our text prompt into a Transformer (like the ones we learned about in Module 3) to convert the text into an embedding vector. We then feed this embedding into the U-Net alongside the noisy image. This is called **Conditioning**.

The U-Net now predicts the noise *conditioned* on the fact that the image should contain a cat.

## The Problem: Ignoring the Prompt

However, researchers noticed a massive problem. The U-Net is lazy. If it can easily predict the noise just by looking at the noisy pixels, it will completely ignore the text prompt! It will generate a beautiful, photorealistic image... of a car, instead of a cat.

To force the U-Net to listen to us, we use a brilliant mathematical trick called **Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)**.

## The CFG Trick

During the reverse process, we don't just run the U-Net once. We run it *twice* at every step:
1. **Unconditional Pass**: We ask the U-Net to predict the noise *without* showing it the text prompt.
2. **Conditional Pass**: We ask the U-Net to predict the noise *with* the text prompt ("A cute cat").

We now have two vectors. By calculating the difference between them, we find the exact mathematical direction that points toward "cat-ness". We then take that difference, multiply it by a massive scalar (the **CFG Scale**), and add it to our prediction.

We literally take the "cat" signal and turn the volume up to 11!

## Finding the Balance

Play with the **CFG Scale** slider above. 

If CFG is too low, the model ignores you. If CFG is perfectly balanced (usually around 7.5), the model perfectly executes your prompt. 

But if you turn the CFG scale too high (e.g., 20), you force the math so hard in one direction that the image literally breaks down, becoming over-saturated and "deep-fried."
